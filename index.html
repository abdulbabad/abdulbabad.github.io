About

Scholar-practitioner in higher and postsecondary education

I am a higher education researcher and academic advisor with roughly twelve years of experience supporting college students. I recently completed my Ed.D. in Higher and Postsecondary Education at Teachers College, Columbia University, where I defended my dissertation in October 2026 (degree conferral expected February 2027). I currently serve as an Integrated Advisor at LaGuardia Community College, CUNY.

My research examines how people and technology shape one another in higher education, and how emotion and identity shape teaching and advising. My doctoral training paired higher education with coursework in learning technologies and educational data mining, quantitative and qualitative research methods, and the psychological and philosophical foundations of education. My dissertation, a narrative inquiry with six instructors, developed a framework I call pedagogical coping to explain how instructors navigated the abrupt shift to emergency remote teaching. Looking ahead, I am bringing natural language processing and other computational methods to questions of practice: what advising notes and open-ended survey responses can tell institutions about how to support students, especially in community colleges.

My path into this work began at a community college. As a first-generation college graduate, I moved from Hudson County Community College to Rutgers University, the University of Connecticut, and Teachers College. That route shapes how I advise students and the questions I bring to research.

I welcome conversations about research collaboration, academic advising, and student success.

Dissertation

Teaching as Coping: Emotion, Identity, and Pedagogical Adaptation in the Shift to Emergency Remote Instruction

Ed.D. dissertation, Teachers College, Columbia University (defended October 2026). Sponsor: Professor Noah Drezner.

The COVID-19 pandemic pushed higher education instructors into an abrupt, unplanned move from face-to-face to online teaching. Most research on that disruption has focused on students. This study turned to instructors themselves, asking what emotions they experienced, whether those emotions helped or hindered their transition, and how emotion figured in their teaching.

Using a narrative inquiry informed qualitative design, I interviewed six higher education instructors and collected reflective journal entries over the course of the study. Schlossberg's Theory of Transition served as an organizing lens for the interview protocol and initial analysis.

Pedagogical coping. The central finding is an emergent framework in which instructors responded to disruption not by retreating from instruction but by reworking it, using pedagogical decision-making itself as the main site for processing emotional strain, reasserting professional identity, and sustaining a sense of purpose.

The framework comprises five interrelated themes: coping as teaching identity, teaching mindset, emotional labor, teaching adaptation, and supporting conditions (instructor well-being, institutional context, and peer support). Across participants, emotion and pedagogical action were mutually constitutive rather than separate, sequential experiences.

The study treats COVID-era emergency remote teaching as one case of broader instructional disruption. It argues that pedagogical coping is legitimate, generative professional labor that institutions should recognize when designing support for instructors, and it points toward extending the framework to other disruptions and testing it as a measurable construct.
